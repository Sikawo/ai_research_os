# Tired Brilliant Outsider Test

## Purpose

Use a tired reviewer: a scientifically sophisticated, highly capable reviewer
who may be outside the applicant's immediate specialty and is evaluating many
strong applications under realistic cognitive and attention constraints. This
is not a "dumb reviewer" test and is not a substitute for domain-expert review.
Never simplify by replacing precise field language with generic language,
removing necessary nuance, or lowering the scientific standard.

The test asks whether a document remains understandable, compelling, and
memorable when the reviewer cannot devote unlimited rereading time. Its central
reconstruction question is:

> **Can a brilliant scientist outside the immediate field reconstruct the
> applicant's central argument from the title, headings, emphasized sentences,
> figures, and captions alone—and then remember the central idea five minutes
> later?**

The test has six distinct axes:

1. full-read narrative and logical comprehension;
2. importance and impact comprehension;
3. logical simplicity and organization;
4. 10-second, 60-second, and five-minute-memory reconstruction;
5. rapid-skim and visual-narrative comprehension;
6. skim-to-full-text consistency.

A document may pass one axis and fail another. Report the axes separately rather
than averaging them into a vague readability judgment.

## Relationship to argument readiness

For eligible proposals and research plans/statements, the canonical motivation,
focus, and readiness checks are in
`plugins/review-tools/skills/application-review/references/core/scientific_argument_gate.md`. Use its source-only
Cold Reader procedure and retain every inference that fills an unstated gap.
The full-read argument pass and this summary-surface pass remain distinct;
body text must not rescue a failed skim pass. Record context exposure when
isolation is unavailable. The time horizons below are simulated review lenses,
not measured human recall. Repeat on the actual final source after polish.

## Review preconditions: source and context

Before reading for strategy, register the current authoritative source, its
fingerprint, the user's current/final designation, and the applicable content
and layout authorities from `artifact_versioning_policy.md`. Perform the first
reconstruction from that source alone before consulting prior scores, author
explanations, earlier assistant prose, rejected suggestions, or older drafts.
Record the context condition as `SOURCE_ONLY` or `CONTEXT_EXPOSED`; never
present an exposed-context pass as independent. Reconcile findings with the
active decision ledger only after this first pass.

A material source change invalidates the prior reconstruction and triggers a new
source-only pass. Conversation persistence, a repeated score, or an earlier
assistant recommendation is not evidence about the current document.

## A. Full-Read Narrative and Logical Comprehension

After one realistic reading, determine whether the outsider can explain:

- the central hypothesis or organizing question;
- why each Aim or Project exists;
- how the Aims or Projects connect;
- what is core versus exploratory or decision-gated;
- what the applicant uniquely owns;
- why the work belongs at the target institute;
- which terms, sentences, or transitions create avoidable cognitive load;
- what would remain in memory the next morning.

The goal is lower reviewer cognitive load while preserving field precision,
applicant-owned concepts, causal qualification, and scientific nuance.

## B. Importance and Impact Comprehension

Evaluate whether a scientifically sophisticated non-specialist can understand
why the work matters, not merely what the work will do.

Check whether the reader can identify:

- the larger scientific, medical, technological, or societal problem;
- why the unresolved knowledge gap matters;
- what remains impossible to understand, predict, measure, or eventually change
  while the gap remains unresolved;
- what directly changes in scientific knowledge if the work succeeds;
- what future research, technology, diagnosis, prevention, or treatment the work
  could enable;
- whether any longer-term medical or societal potential is clearly distinguished
  from the direct scientific outcome;
- whether the mechanism-to-consequence or discovery-to-impact chain is explicit
  rather than assumed;
- whether the importance is specific, memorable, and proportionate to the
  evidence;
- whether the importance can be summarized accurately in one sentence after one
  reading;
- whether the proposal explains why the problem is timely when a genuine
  timeliness argument exists.

Run an opening-only significance pass, expressed here as the broader
motivation/value pass, before using the rest of each unit.
Read only the heading plus the opening one to three sentences. For a research
document, test the program overview, each Aim/project, each major accomplishment
or future-program block, and each major argumentative unit that introduces a
new question, direction, or consequential claim. For a cover letter, test the
letter opening and every research/evidence block. For an evaluative letter,
test each major evaluative block. Ask whether an adjacent reader can retrieve
the motivating problem or question and why resolving it matters before methods,
dense evidence, qualifications, or caveats. Classify each unit `EARLY`,
`DELAYED`, `MISSING`, or `NOT_ASSESSABLE` and quote or locate the supporting
surface. A detailed body cannot rescue a failed opening result. Do not demand a
new significance sentence from a detail paragraph that introduces no new
argument, and do not manufacture repetitive "this matters because" formulas.
Later body text may establish importance, but it cannot rescue this placement pass.

Do not require every basic-science proposal to claim immediate medical or
societal impact. Strong fundamental scientific importance is sufficient when it
is concrete and clearly explained. Flag both under-explained significance and
inflated, generic, or unsupported impact claims.

Use these distinctions:

- `Direct scientific impact`: what this work itself will establish or change;
- `Enabling impact`: what new questions, measurements, models, or interventions
  become possible because of that result;
- `Potential medical or societal impact`: a longer-term consequence that may
  follow through additional steps and should not be presented as immediate.

Also apply a counterfactual test: if this work were not done, what important
problem, ambiguity, or limitation would remain?

## C. Logical Simplicity and Cumulative-Edit Regression

Evaluate logical simplicity as a hard gate independent from grammar, tone,
sentence polish, density, and skim performance. A polished document fails this
gate when the reader must reconstruct an avoidably branched or delayed argument.

### Document architecture

From the current source, record:

- the primary message in one sentence;
- the shortest defensible main chain of premises and consequences;
- the job of every major section and paragraph;
- any premise introduced after the claim that depends on it;
- branches that are optional, duplicative, or unrelated to the main chain;
- duplicated rationales or applications;
- defensive caveats or reassurance that obscure the claim they qualify; and
- any paragraph performing multiple incompatible jobs.

The gate passes only when the hierarchy is recoverable, each paragraph has one
dominant job, necessary qualifications remain attached to the claim they bound,
and the main chain can be stated without inventing a missing link. Simplify by
moving, replacing, compressing, or deleting existing material before adding new
prose. Necessary scientific nuance is not clutter.

### Candidate and integrated-bundle comparison

Freeze the current source as the comparison baseline. Evaluate each proposed
edit locally **and** evaluate the fully integrated edit bundle. The cumulative
bundle fails when, compared with the baseline, it:

- makes the primary message harder to state accurately;
- introduces more concepts, branches, or qualifications than it resolves;
- increases rereading without an essential scientific or factual benefit;
- blurs section or paragraph jobs;
- duplicates motivation, significance, fit, or reassurance; or
- protects against remote objections at the cost of ordinary comprehension.

Do not let several individually defensible edits bypass this regression test.
Report the baseline, the integrated candidate, the regression mechanism, and
the smallest sufficient rollback or restructuring.

When an isolated reader is available, run this comparison blind: give the
reader the baseline and integrated candidate separately, without labels,
intended answer, prior scores, edit rationale, or conversation history. Ask the
reader to reconstruct the primary message, central contribution/delta, future
direction, main reason to care, and paragraph/section jobs for each artifact;
then compare accuracy, branch count, rereading, and qualifier burden. If true
isolation is unavailable, perform the same source-bounded reconstruction before
reviewing the edit rationale and disclose `CONTEXT_EXPOSED / NON_BLIND
FALLBACK`. A non-blind comparison must not be reported as blind.

For cover letters, apply the test especially strictly. Each body paragraph must
retain one clear role and a visible link between target need and evidence. The
integrated letter must not become a compressed research statement or a catalog
of applications, methods, caveats, reassurance, fit claims, and credentials.
Test both the local edit and the complete letter after all accepted edits.

## D. Reconstruction, Rapid-Skim, and Visual-Narrative Test

Simulate a tired senior reviewer who initially inspects only the available
high-information surfaces before deciding whether to read closely.

For scientific proposals, research statements, and research plans, inspect only:

- title;
- section, Aim, and Project headings;
- emphasized or visually dominant key sentences, when present;
- graphical abstract, cartoon, model, or overview schematic;
- figures in document order;
- figure titles and captions;
- boxed take-home messages or other visually dominant summary elements.

For another document type, adapt the list to its legitimate summary surfaces;
do not invent figure requirements for a document that is not a scientific plan.

Do not use detailed body-text explanations to rescue this pass. Record exactly
which surfaces were available and reviewed. If rendered figures are unavailable,
do not infer visual clarity from filenames, captions, alt text, or extracted text;
mark visual judgments as `Not assessable from authorized sources`.

From these surfaces alone, determine whether the reviewer can correctly infer:

- `Problem`: the important problem being addressed;
- `Gap`: what remains unknown or unresolved;
- `Applicant insight / discovery`: what the applicant has contributed that
  changes how the problem is viewed;
- `Central question / hypothesis`: the organizing scientific question;
- `Distinctive solution`: what is unusual or applicant-specific about the
  proposed way of solving it;
- `Feasibility / credibility`: why the approach is reasonable rather than
  merely clever;
- `Payoff`: what directly changes if the work succeeds.

The target reaction is: "I had not thought about the problem that way, but once
explained, it makes sense." Test this as `distinctive + reasonable`. Explicitly
flag work that is distinctive but implausible, feasible but incremental,
interesting but unsupported, or technically credible but conceptually
unmemorable.

### Three Time-Horizon Reconstruction Tests

#### 10-second test

Using only the title, major headings, and major visual or visuals, record
whether the reviewer can identify:

- what kind of scientist or applicant this is;
- the core scientific problem;
- the central organizing idea.

#### 60-second test

Using only the complete set of summary surfaces, record whether the reviewer can
explain the problem, gap, applicant insight, central question, distinctive
solution, feasibility, and payoff. Detailed body text must not rescue this pass.

#### 5-minute memory test

After mentally closing the application, record in one sentence the applicant's
distinctive scientific idea or the single memorable program-level logic. Also
record what is most likely to remain in memory and what is most likely to be
forgotten or confused.

### Visual Reconstruction Rule

> **A major figure should communicate an argument, not merely illustrate
> content. Whenever possible, the central argument of the page should remain
> reconstructable from the figure and its caption without requiring detailed
> body text.**

For an overview figure or cartoon:

> **The overview figure should allow a reviewer to reconstruct the program-level
> scientific logic without reading the detailed text.**

Assess whether the visual system collectively makes visible the problem, model,
known evidence, applicant finding, unresolved question, proposed work, and
expected conceptual advance. Do not require every figure to contain every
element. Require each figure to perform one clear rhetorical and scientific job.

### Figure 1 role and program-coverage test

Classify Figure 1 before judging what it must contain:

- `PROGRAM_OVERVIEW`;
- `CENTRAL_MODEL`;
- `ANCHOR_EVIDENCE`; or
- `OTHER_JUSTIFIED_ROLE` with a short explanation.

Independently build a program-to-surface coverage map: enumerate the
program-defining pillars, Aims, or branches and map each one to its dominant
summary surface (Figure 1, another figure, headings, or an explicitly designated
textual overview). For every mapping, record the surface and evidence status:
established evidence, applicant finding,
hypothesis/model, proposed work, expected outcome, or future implication.

If Figure 1 is the designated `PROGRAM_OVERVIEW`, every major branch must appear
there at the correct status. A missing branch fails program coverage even when
it appears later in body text. If Figure 1 has another legitimate role, do not
force every branch into it; require another explicit dominant surface to
integrate the whole program. If no surface integrates the program, report the
coverage gap and the smallest sufficient correction.

### Figure Question Test

For every substantive figure or proposed visual, ask:

> **What reviewer question does this figure answer?**

Strong answers include: What is the whole program? Is this phenomenon real?
Has the applicant established feasibility? What distinguishes this model from
the conventional model? How do the Aims or Projects connect? What is the
mechanistic model? What happens if the main path fails? Which result belongs to
the applicant? Why does this work matter?

"It looks nice," "the prose already says this," "every page should have a
figure," and "it shows data without changing reviewer understanding" are weak
answers. If no meaningful reviewer question is answered, recommend removal,
replacement, or no figure.

### Figure Take-Home Test

Require each substantive figure title or caption to state its evidence-calibrated
scientific take-home message, not merely list visible objects, methods, or the
experimental system. A descriptive caption such as "measurements in cells" is
insufficient when authorized evidence supports a precise conclusion. Never
fabricate a conclusion, overstate certainty, or let the caption exceed the
authorized evidence.

### Summary-Figure and Cartoon Assessment

For each overview schematic, cartoon, or summary figure, assess:

- which reviewer question it answers and whether that question is important at
  its location;
- whether it has a clear single purpose;
- whether its take-home message is visible without reading dense prose;
- whether its title or caption communicates the evidence-calibrated scientific
  conclusion rather than merely describing objects or methods;
- whether it distinguishes known facts, preliminary findings, hypotheses,
  proposed experiments, expected outcomes, and future implications;
- whether arrows, labels, symbols, colors, and panel relationships have
  unambiguous meanings;
- whether it is legible at the size at which the reviewer will actually see it;
- whether it appears early enough to orient the reader before the difficult text
  it is supposed to clarify;
- whether it appears so early that necessary concepts have not yet been
  introduced;
- whether it duplicates prose without adding structure;
- whether it oversimplifies or visually implies causality, certainty, sequence,
  scale, or completeness that the evidence does not support.

### Figure Position, Sequence, and Visual Story

Evaluate rhetorical order separately from mechanical numbering correctness.
Ask whether:

- the first visual establishes the problem, model, or organizing concept needed
  for later figures;
- subsequent figures build a cumulative argument rather than appearing as an
  unordered collection of data;
- each figure appears near the text or section where it is needed;
- a figure is introduced before the reader needs information contained in it;
- a later figure is required to understand an earlier section;
- the figure sequence matches the Aim or Project sequence, or any deviation has
  a clear communicative reason;
- the overview figure and detailed figures use consistent terminology, symbols,
  and causal direction;
- the most important figure receives appropriate prominence rather than being
  buried after secondary material;
- the visual sequence communicates the same story as the title, abstract, and
  body text.

A document can pass the `Figure Order and In-Text Citation Audit` and still fail
this visual-narrative test. Correct numerical order is not evidence of effective
rhetorical order.

### Skim-to-Full-Text Consistency

Compare the impression formed during the skim-only pass with the conclusion of
the full-read pass. Flag:

- a title or abstract that promises a different study from the figures;
- a cartoon that presents a hypothesis as an established mechanism;
- figures that make a secondary finding look like the central contribution;
- a visually prominent medical claim that is more tentative in the body;
- a key concept present in the body but absent from all summary surfaces;
- a summary surface that is accurate individually but collectively creates a
  misleading overall impression;
- an important Aim or Project that disappears during skim reading;
- a central concept that exists only in body text;
- figures that imply a one-project program when the body defines two or more;
- inconsistent terminology or causal direction between the overview and
  detailed figures;
- a skim-only applicant identity that differs from the full text;
- a memorable idea that is not the most important scientific contribution.

Report the most likely incorrect conclusion a tired reviewer could form from the
summary surfaces alone.

### Tired-Reviewer Optimization Is Not Density Minimization

> **Tired-reviewer optimization is not synonymous with minimizing text or
> maximizing white space.**

A scientifically dense page may pass when visual hierarchy and summary surfaces
carry the argument and permit selective dives into detail. A sparse page may
fail when the central idea requires every paragraph, novelty is not surfaced,
or figures do not carry an argument. Evaluate reconstructability, scientific
precision, and hierarchy—not superficial sparseness.

### Word Page-Layout Readability

When a Word file will be created or materially reformatted, apply
`word_output_format_policy.md` and assess the rendered pages when tooling permits.
Check whether:

- justified body text creates excessive word-spacing rivers or stretched short
  lines;
- line and paragraph spacing provide sufficient visual separation;
- left-aligned headings, figure captions, narrow figure-side text, and
  figure-internal text remain easy to scan;
- paragraph length and page density support realistic tired reading;
- each visual anchor performs a clear explanatory job rather than satisfying a
  quota;
- the figure/placeholder and caption remain together and legible;
- page-bottom headings, widows, orphans, and unstable floating objects appear;
- the preferred layout stays inside official page and template constraints.

For a scientific research plan, also inspect its page-level visual plan. Each
substantive page must record the page or section, narrative job, main reviewer
doubt, whether a visual is recommended, visual function, figure or placeholder
type, one-sentence take-home message, evidence/provenance boundary, approximate
page-area budget, and a reason when a visual is omitted.

If rendered pages are unavailable, report these judgments as `NOT ASSESSABLE`
rather than assuming that extracted text will paginate cleanly.

## Required Verdicts

Report separate verdicts for:

- Full-read comprehension: `CLEAR / PARTLY CLEAR / UNCLEAR`;
- Importance and impact comprehension: `CLEAR / PARTLY CLEAR / UNCLEAR /
  OVERCLAIMED`;
- Opening motivation/value retrieval by applicable unit: `EARLY / DELAYED /
  MISSING / NOT_ASSESSABLE`;
- Logical simplicity and organization: `PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL`;
- Cumulative-edit regression against the current baseline: `PASS / FAIL / NOT
  APPLICABLE`;
- 10-second reconstruction: `CLEAR / PARTLY CLEAR / UNCLEAR / NOT ASSESSABLE`;
- 60-second reconstruction: `CLEAR / PARTLY CLEAR / UNCLEAR / MISLEADING /
  NOT ASSESSABLE`;
- 5-minute memory: `DISTINCTIVE / GENERIC / CONFUSED / NOT ASSESSABLE`;
- Rapid-skim comprehension: `CLEAR / PARTLY CLEAR / MISLEADING / NOT ASSESSABLE`;
- Visual narrative: `EFFECTIVE / PARTLY EFFECTIVE / INEFFECTIVE / NOT
  ASSESSABLE`;
- Figure-by-figure reviewer-question test: `PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL / NOT
  APPLICABLE / NOT ASSESSABLE`;
- Figure 1 role: `PROGRAM_OVERVIEW / CENTRAL_MODEL / ANCHOR_EVIDENCE /
  OTHER_JUSTIFIED_ROLE / NOT APPLICABLE / NOT ASSESSABLE`;
- Program-to-dominant-surface coverage: `COMPLETE / PARTIAL / MISSING /
  NOT APPLICABLE / NOT ASSESSABLE`;
- Skim-to-full-text consistency: `CONSISTENT / PARTLY CONSISTENT /
  INCONSISTENT / NOT ASSESSABLE`.

Always report the most memorable scientific idea, the most likely tired-reviewer
misinterpretation, what will probably be forgotten or confused after five
minutes, and the smallest safe corrective action. For every failed or partial
axis, identify the exact surface, location, and reviewer consequence. Do not
recommend adding a new figure when repositioning, simplifying, relabeling, or
changing the summary function of an existing figure would solve the problem.
